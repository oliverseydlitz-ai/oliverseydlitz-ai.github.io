Range balls vs premium balls: what changes in your numbers

By Oliver Seydlitz · Updated 23 September 2026

More than most people expect. Range balls don't just fly a bit shorter. They change distance by different amounts for different clubs, they roughly double or quadruple your spread, and with a wedge they can even go further on half the spin. If you hit range balls on Tuesday and your own ball on Saturday, the difference between those sessions is mostly the ball.

What a robot found

The cleanest test of this used a swing robot, which matters because a robot has no bad days. It swung the same way every time, once with medium-worn range balls and once with premium balls. Here is what changed with the range balls:

ClubDistanceSpread and spin
Driver12 yards shorterAbout twice as wide side to side, and the shot-to-shot scatter 3–4 times larger
5-iron8 yards shorterAbout 4 times the range
Pitching wedge4 yards longerSpin roughly halved

Read the spread column again. A robot with zero swing variation got two to four times the dispersion just from changing the ball. Any dispersion number worked out from range balls is measuring the ball as much as it's measuring you.

Why that matters for your numbers

Dispersion. The whole reason to track your spread is to find the misses that cost shots: the ones that go out of bounds or into trees. With range balls, that spread gets inflated by roughly the same amount as the real gap between a golfer who shoots 80 and one who shoots 100. You can't see your own improvement under that much extra noise.

Gapping. You'd think a range ball just shifts everything shorter by the same amount, so the gaps between clubs would survive. The wedge row says otherwise: the driver lost 12 yards, the 5-iron 8, and the wedge gained 4. The ball doesn't shorten your bag evenly, so the distance between two clubs off range balls isn't the distance on the course.

Spin. Half the spin on a wedge is a different shot, not a slightly worse one. And on a launch monitor, spin is already the shakiest number you get, so range-ball spin is noise on top of noise.

Mats change things too

Most range balls get hit off mats, which adds a second set of changes:

What changes on a matBy how much
8-iron spinAbout 1,000 to 1,300 rpm less than off grass
Launch angleAbout 1 to 2° higher
Where it hits the faceHigher up, around the 8th–9th groove instead of the 4th–5th
CarryAround 10 yards longer, as an artefact

Treat those as rough: they come from industry testing, and the sizes aren't well pinned down. The one nobody has put a number on at all is the most important: a mat hides fat strikes. It's hard, so the club bounces into the ball instead of digging into the ground behind it. A strike that would have taken a big divot on grass can come off a mat looking almost normal.

So what should you do?

Range balls are still useful. They're just a different measuring instrument, and you should treat them that way.

  • Write down the ball every session. ShotLab asks for the ball and the surface on every import for exactly this reason. It's the one piece of information that decides whether two sessions can be compared.
  • Only compare like with like. Range ball against range ball, your own ball against your own ball, mat against mat. A range session against a premium one tells you about the balls.
  • Build your yardages on your own ball. If you're going to club off a number on the course, get it from the ball you play.
  • Use range sessions for things the ball affects less. Club speed is the same whatever you hit. Strike work still makes sense, as long as you only compare it with other range sessions.
  • Take range-ball spread with a pinch of salt. It's still worth watching against other range sessions, but for a dispersion number you'd trust on the course, give it a session with a premium or RPT ball.

Sources

  • Golf.com, Range ball vs premium ball robot test — industry testing on a swing robot, reported rather than peer-reviewed.
  • Broadie and Ko (2009), A simulation model to analyze the impact of distance and direction on golf scores, Winter Simulation Conference — the 80-golfer and 100-golfer spread.
