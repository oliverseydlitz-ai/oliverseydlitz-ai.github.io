# Range balls vs premium balls: what changes in your numbers

More than you'd think. Range balls don't just fly a bit shorter. They change distance by different amounts for different clubs, they make your spread two to four times wider, and with a wedge they can even go *further* with half the spin. If you hit range balls on Tuesday and your own ball on Saturday, most of the difference is the ball.

[[photo: a bucket of range balls next to a sleeve of premium balls on the mat]]

## What a robot found

The best test of this used a swing robot, which is useful because a robot has no bad days. It swung the same way every time, once with worn range balls and once with premium balls. Here's what changed with the range balls:

| Club | Distance | Spread and spin |
|---|---|---|
| Driver | 12 yards shorter | About twice as wide side to side, and the shot-to-shot scatter 3–4 times larger |
| 5-iron | 8 yards shorter | About 4 times the range |
| Pitching wedge | 4 yards *longer* | Spin roughly halved |

Read that again. **A robot that swings identically every time got two to four times the spread just from changing the ball.** Any spread you measure with range balls is telling you about the ball as much as about you.

## Why that matters for your numbers

**Spread.** The point of tracking your spread is to find the misses that cost shots: out of bounds, in the trees. Range balls add about as much spread as the whole difference between an 80 shooter and a 100 shooter. You can't see yourself improving through that.

**Gapping.** You'd think range balls just knock the same few yards off every club, so the gaps stay the same. They don't: the driver lost 12 yards, the 5-iron 8, and the wedge *gained* 4. So your gaps on range balls aren't your gaps on the course.

**Spin.** Half the spin on a wedge is a different shot, not a slightly worse one. And spin is already the shakiest number on a launch monitor, so range-ball spin is even less use.

## Mats change things too

Most range balls get hit off mats, and mats change things too:

| What changes on a mat | By how much |
|---|---|
| 8-iron spin | About 1,000 to 1,300 rpm less than off grass |
| Launch angle | About 1 to 2° higher |
| Where it hits the face | Higher up, around the 8th–9th groove instead of the 4th–5th |
| Carry | Reads around 10 yards longer than it really is |

Treat those numbers as rough, they come from industry testing. The most important one has no number at all: **a mat hides fat shots.** The club bounces off the mat into the ball instead of digging in behind it. A shot that would have been a big chunk on grass can look almost normal off a mat.

## So what should you do?

Range balls are still useful. Just don't treat them like your real ball.

- **Note the ball every session.** ShotLab asks for the ball and the surface on every import for this reason. It decides whether two sessions can be compared at all.
- **Compare like with like.** Range balls against range balls, your ball against your ball, mat against mat. A range session against a premium one just tells you about the balls.
- **Build your yardages on your own ball.** If you're going to club off a number on the course, get it from the ball you play.
- **Use range sessions for things the ball doesn't change much.** Club speed is the same whatever you hit. Strike work is fine too, as long as you compare it with other range sessions.
- **Take range-ball spread with a pinch of salt.** It's worth watching against other range sessions, but for a spread you'd trust on the course, hit a session with a premium or RPT ball.
