Drill library and fault→cause logic

Verdict first

The drills are good. The numbers they hang off are not, and one of them is backwards.

The drill prescriptions in FaultEngine and CoachingMode hold up well — the fault-to-cause mappings match mainstream instruction, and the drills themselves are real, specific, and correctly matched to their faults. That's the expensive part to get right, and it's right.

StatusAll six corrections below are now implemented and pushed to main. The register is kept as the sourcing for those values — the numbers in the code came from here, so this is the document to re-read before changing any of them. Each record carries what shipped.

What doesn't hold up is the measurement layer underneath: the tour benchmark table has a sign error on the most-viewed metric in golf, the face-to-path calculation is missing a term from the D-plane model it cites, and every per-shot fault threshold is tighter than the launch monitor's own margin of error. Those are all narrow, fixable defects — but while they're in place the app is confidently reporting numbers that aren't true.

Register of corrections

Six findings, each traced to the line of code that carries it.

Wrong sign Fixed BENCH-AOA

The app tells golfers PGA pros hit up on the driver. They hit down.

Benchmarks.DATA lists the PGA Tour driver attack angle as aa:3.0. The Trackman tour average is −1.3° — descending. The app is off by 4.3° and in the wrong direction, on the single metric golfers check most.

The likely origin: +3.0° is the LPGA Tour average, and +2° to +5° is the range the PGA Tour's longest hitters use. Two true facts, attached to the wrong row.

Do not "fix" the drill

The coaching advice built on this is correct: a positive attack angle really does produce more carry. Trackman's own optimizer shows a 90 mph swing going from −5° to +5° gains roughly 30 yards of carry (launch 10°→16°, spin 3,100→2,200 rpm), and −2°→+3° is worth about 12 yards. So driver-negative-aa and its drills stay. Only the benchmark label is wrong — and the fix is to stop calling +3.0 the tour average and start calling it the target.

Systematic Fixed BENCH-DRIFT

Attack angles run shallow and smash factors run low across the whole table

The driver row isn't isolated. Every club in the table reports a shallower attack angle than the tour average, and every smash factor sits low — so a golfer comparing themselves to "PGA" is measured against a softer standard than the real one.

Math Fixed MATH-F2P

face-to-path is under-reported by 15% on driver and 25% on irons

facePath() computes launchDirection − clubPath. But the app's own slice description cites the D-plane rule that start direction is mostly face — and that rule is exactly what makes the subtraction incomplete. Start direction is a weighted blend of face and path, so recovering face-to-path means dividing the weight back out:

// D-plane: launch direction is a weighted blend of face and path
launchDir = k·face + (1−k)·path   k = 0.85 driver, 0.75 iron

// solve for face, then subtract path
face = (launchDir − (1−k)·path) / k
face−path = (launchDir − path) / k

// the app omits the divisor entirely
app = launchDir − path  → understates by exactly k

The consequence is a systematic blind spot at the threshold. slice fires at facePath(s) > 5, so:

ClubTrue face-to-pathApp readsSlice rule
Driver5.0°4.25°misses it
Iron5.0°3.75°misses it
Driver7.1°6.0°fires
Iron8.0°6.0°fires

A golfer with a genuine 5° open face — a real, visible slice — is told their face-to-path is fine. And because k differs by club, the same error is 10 percentage points worse on irons than on driver, so the app is least accurate on the clubs hit most.

Two lines nearby need the same correction: the slice description hardcodes "~75% of starting direction is determined by face angle" as a universal figure. It's 75% for irons and 85% for driver — loft is what moves the ratio.

Precision Fixed THRESH-NOISE

Per-shot thresholds are finer than the launch monitor can resolve

Good news first: the MLM2PRO measures club path and attack angle with radar and dual cameras rather than inferring them from ball flight, so the fault rules rest on real data. Independent comparison against a Foresight GCQuad puts the mean absolute error at 1.05° for attack angle and 1.19° for club path.

That number is larger than several thresholds it feeds. iron-shallow-aa fires on any single shot with attackAngle > −0.5. A shot genuinely struck at −1.5° sits inside the instrument's error band for that verdict — so the fault fires on measurement noise, on individual shots, with no aggregation.

Every rule in PER_SHOT_RULES evaluates shots one at a time and reports any that trip. With ±1.2° of noise on a 20-shot session, a handful of false positives per session is the expected outcome, not the exception.

The fix is statistical, not cosmetic

Require a fault to clear the threshold by more than the instrument error, and require it across a minimum sample — a mean over ≥5 shots of that club, or a count of affected shots above what noise alone would produce. A fault that appears on 9 of 12 seven-irons is a swing pattern; the same fault on 2 of 12 is the radar.

Gap Fixed GAP-SPINLOFT

Spin loft is absent — the one number that explains strike quality

Spin loft is dynamic loft minus attack angle: the angle between where the face points and where the club is travelling. It is the primary driver of both spin and smash factor, and the app models neither it nor anything equivalent.

This matters because it resolves an ambiguity the app currently can't. When poor-contact fires on low smash, the cause is either off-centre strike or excessive spin loft — mechanically different faults needing opposite drills. Without spin loft the app guesses, and its causes list for that fault names only strike-location issues.

Spin rises with spin loft only up to about 45°; past that the ball slides up the face, friction is lost and spin falls again. Rapsodo doesn't export dynamic loft, so spin loft can't be computed exactly — but attack angle plus spin rate and launch angle bound it well enough to separate "you missed the middle" from "you're adding loft through impact."

Verified OK-DRILLS

What checks out

Worth stating plainly, because most of the coaching content survives scrutiny:

  • Driver benchmarks other than AoA — 1.48 smash, 167 mph ball, 10.9° launch, 275 yd carry all match Trackman exactly.
  • Driver spin threshold — the 3,500 rpm trigger and the 2,686 rpm tour average cited in the fault text are both correct.
  • Fault → cause mappings — over-the-top for slice, strong grip for hook, early extension for thin contact: all standard and correctly assigned.
  • The drills themselves — tee gate, feet-together, headcover-behind-ball, towel-under-arm, divot-after-the-ball. Real drills, correctly matched, well described.
  • Attack-angle targets — +2° to +5° driver, −2° to −5° irons. Sound as targets, whatever the benchmark table says about tour averages.
  • Smash ceiling — 1.50 is the COR-limited maximum; the app never claims above it.

The practice science

The app's practice plans rest on a claim the research does not support as strongly as golf coaching believes it does.

PracticePlan.generate() allocates minutes by fault severity, and the surrounding copy echoes the standard modern-coaching line: blocked practice is the enemy, random practice transfers. That line is far shakier than its confidence suggests, and getting this right changes what the app should tell people to do.

Random vs. blocked practice (contextual interference)

Contested

The textbook claim — random practice hurts acquisition but helps retention and transfer — is robust in laboratory settings and close to absent in applied sport settings. A systematic review and meta-analysis of sports practice found no statistically significant difference between blocked and random at post-acquisition (d = 0.1), retention (d = −0.159) or transfer (d = −0.243). Only 25 of 75 pooled outcomes favoured random practice; twice as many contradicted it. Across 183 outcomes, just 20% agreed with the predicted effect.

This is genuinely disputed rather than settled: a 2024 meta-analysis in Scientific Reports reaches the opposite conclusion, that high contextual interference does improve retention. Effects also appear larger in older adults than younger ones.

Golf-specific putting studies do favour random practice, and it plausibly helps. But "blocked practice is the #1 transfer killer" is a coaching slogan, not a finding. Don't build the product's central practice claim on it.

External focus of attention

Well supported

The most actionable finding here, and the cheapest to apply: cue wording changes performance. Internal cues ("keep your wrists straight") interfere with automaticity and degrade accuracy; external cues ("swing toward the target line") improve consistency and reduce movement variability. There's a distance effect too — focusing on ball flight and landing point beats focusing on the club.

Recent reviews question whether the advantage is as universal as early summaries implied, so it isn't absolute. But the direction is consistent and the cost of applying it is zero.

This is a direct problem for the current drill text. CoachingMode.TIPS is almost entirely internal: "keep your head still", "maintain spine angle", "check for over-strong grip". The drills in FaultEngine are noticeably better — "avoid hitting the towel", "swing without clipping the tee" are external by construction.

Differential learning

Promising

Deliberately varying the movement itself — different stances, tempos, ball positions, never repeating the same swing — rather than varying only the club or target. In golf putting it produced a more structured mental representation than either random or blocked schedules. In comparable studies blocked practice won during acquisition while differential groups transferred better and reported higher motivation.

Fits a launch-monitor app unusually well: the monitor supplies the feedback that makes exploratory variation interpretable instead of merely random.

Feedback frequency

Little evidence

The intuition that constant feedback creates dependence, and that reducing it forces error-estimation, is widespread. A meta-analysis found substantial heterogeneity and no significant effect of reduced feedback frequency at any time point, concluding robust evidence is lacking. Self-controlled feedback — letting the learner choose when to see it — shows an advantage more often, attributed to autonomy and error-estimation, but not reliably.

Relevant because a launch monitor is a maximum-frequency feedback device. There's no good evidence that hiding numbers helps — but letting the golfer choose when to look is the better-supported design, and it's a UI decision the app already controls.

Practice dosage and difficulty

Consistent

The most consistent practical guidance across sources: three focused sessions of ~50 balls beat one session of 150. Faster than one shot per 20 seconds is exercise, not practice. Tasks should be calibrated so the golfer succeeds around 70% of the time — hard enough to demand adjustment, easy enough to sustain. Warm-up runs wedges first, 4–5 balls per club, before anything full-power. And deliberate practice accounted for roughly 30% of improvement in one golf study — a third of the process, not all of it.

Directly usable: PracticePlan currently outputs minutes only. Ball counts, shot spacing and a success-rate target are all cheap to add and better supported than the schedule ordering it already emphasises.

What to practise, not just how

Fault severity is the wrong ranking. Scoring impact is the right one.

PracticePlan weights time by severity: high|medium|low — a property hardcoded per fault, identical for every golfer. So a session dominated by driver faults allocates most of its time to the driver regardless of whether the driver is actually costing that golfer strokes.

Mark Broadie's strokes-gained work gives the missing weighting. Approach shots explain the largest share of scoring differences between players, and the long game — not putting — accounts for most of the gap between amateurs and professionals. Higher handicaps lose 2–4 shots per round from driving distance alone. The popular intuition that putting is where amateurs bleed strokes is the thing the data most clearly refutes.

ShotLab can't compute true strokes gained: it sees range shots, not rounds, and has no putting or short-game data. But it can stop pretending all faults cost the same. A face-to-path fault on a 7-iron — an approach club — deserves more session time than the same fault on a 3-wood most golfers hit twice a round. Club frequency and club category are both already in the shot data.

Calibration reference

For context when interpreting a user's numbers: the average male amateur drives 224 yards total, a 10-handicap 226.6, scratch to 4.9 handicap 244. Roughly 70% of amateurs average under 250 off the tee. Drawn from Arccos data across ~5M rounds and Shot Scope's user base — on-course totals, not launch-monitor carry, so they are not directly comparable to a range session's carry numbers.

On gapping, the app's yardage book should target 10–15 yards between clubs, built from about 10 shots per club with outliers removed — which is a stricter sample requirement than the current yardage view enforces.
